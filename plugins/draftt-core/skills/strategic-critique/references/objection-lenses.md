# Objection lenses

Run the plan through each lens, then keep only the sharpest objection for the critique. A critique that lists twelve objections is easy to ignore; one that lands the right one gets acted on.

| Lens | The skeptic asks | Signs the plan is weak here |
|------|------------------|-----------------------------|
| **User** | Do these users actually have this problem, badly enough to change what they do? | The user is a category, not a person in a situation. The pain is asserted, not observed. The workaround they use today is never mentioned |
| **Value** | Why would anyone switch from what they do now? | No comparison to the current workaround. The benefit is a feature, not an outcome |
| **Business** | How does this move a number the business cares about? | Goals are activity ("launch", "ship") rather than outcomes. No cost side |
| **Feasibility** | Can we build and run this with the people and systems we have? | Requirements depend on data, integrations, or skills nobody has confirmed |
| **Timing** | Why now, and what happens if we wait a quarter? | No reason it has to happen now. Or a deadline drives scope with no fallback |
| **Sequencing** | Is this the right first step? | The plan builds the expensive part before testing the riskiest assumption |
| **Ethics and accessibility** | Who could this hurt or leave out? | No mention of users with disabilities, low digital confidence, shared devices, or sensitive data. Incentives that reward the business at the user's expense |
| **Measurement** | How will we know it worked, and when would we stop? | No leading signal, no kill criteria, or metrics that would go up even if users were worse off |

## Writing the objection
- Write it as the stakeholder would say it, in one or two sentences.
- Aim it at the riskiest assumption when you can. The best objection and the riskiest assumption are often the same thing seen from two sides.
- Don't attribute it to a real, named person.
